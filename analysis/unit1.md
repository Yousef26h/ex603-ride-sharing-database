### Reflection on Unit 1
Working through Unit 1 taught me that database design is really a series of trade-offs rather than a search for one correct answer. Before this unit, I assumed the schema was just a description of the data, but writing the domains, constraints, and ON DELETE behaviours forced me to think about what could go wrong and decide which failures the database should make impossible. The most valuable moment was realising that a junction table isn't a special construct but simply the natural result of two one-to-many relationships meeting in the middle, which made the whole five-role structure click. I also learned that leaving a rule to the application is not laziness but a deliberate choice when the rule depends on runtime state. If I did this again, I would start with the open questions (nullability, deletion behaviour) before drafting tables, because those decisions shaped everything downstream.


### Modeling justifications
1. **Surrogate keys**: I chose surrogate keys for all tables because no natural attribute is guaranteed to be unique and stable. For example, names are culturally variable and can change over time, so they cannot serve as reliable primary keys. Surrogate keys provide a consistent and efficient way to identify records across the database, ensuring that relationships between tables remain intact even if other attributes change.
2. **Data types**: I selected data types based on the expected range and precision of the data. For instance, I used `NUMERIC(10,2)` for fare amounts to ensure accurate representation of monetary values, avoiding the pitfalls of floating-point arithmetic. The `TIMESTAMP` type was chosen for booking times to capture both date and time information accurately, while `BOOLEAN` was used for the `isActive` attribute to clearly represent the driver's availability status.
3. **Constraints**: I implemented constraints to enforce data integrity and business rules. For example, the `NOT NULL` constraint on critical fields like `RiderID`, `DriverID`, and `fareAmount` ensures that essential information is always present, preventing incomplete records. The `UNIQUE` constraint on badge names in the `DriverBadges` table ensures that each badge is distinct, supporting consistent classification of drivers. Additionally, foreign key constraints maintain referential integrity between related tables, ensuring that trips always reference valid riders and drivers, and that badge awards are linked to existing badges and drivers.
4. **ON DELETE behaviors**: I carefully considered the `ON DELETE` actions for foreign keys to preserve historical data and maintain the integrity of the database. For example, using `RESTRICT` on the `RiderID` and `DriverID` foreign keys in the `Trips` table prevents accidental deletion of riders or drivers that would orphan trip records, which are critical for financial and operational analysis. This decision ensures that the database retains a complete history of trips, even if a rider or driver is no longer active on the platform.
